## 1. Executive Summary & Book Structure

Raymond Murphy's *English Grammar in Use* is organized into **145 units** spanning **16 core grammatical domains**, accompanied by **7 specialized appendices**, a comprehensive diagnostic **Study Guide** (page 326), and an exhaustive alphabetical **Index** (pages 373–380). 

Each unit follows a consistent two-page pedagogical architecture:
- **Left-hand page**: Clear explanations, syntactic rules, grammatical diagrams, and contextualized example sentences broken into alphabetical sub-sections (**A**, **B**, **C**, **D**, **E**).
- **Right-hand page**: Targeted practice exercises directly consolidating each sub-section's rules.

This document integrates the complete **Contents** structure and the back-of-the-book **Index** to provide an exhaustive, cross-referenced mapping of English grammar rules to the specific structural, lexical, and prepositional interference traps encountered by Spanish speakers at the CEFR B2 and C1 levels.

## 2. Master Diagnostic Table: Top Spanish-Interference Traps Mapped to Murphy

The table below details high-frequency B2/C1 errors stemming from Spanish language transfer (*calques, false cognates, prepositional mismatch, and syntactic reordering*), mapped to the exact unit numbers and sub-sections where the rule is explained.

| Common Spanish-Interference Trap & Root Cause | Correct English Rule & Contrastive Structure | Murphy Unit & Sub-section (Index Reference) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **"Depend of / depend from"**<br>*(Calque of depender de)* | **depend on / rely on**<br>English strictly uses *on* for contingency or reliance. Before question-word clauses, *on* is optional: *It depends (on) how much it is.*<br>• ❌ *It depends of the weather.*<br>• ✅ *It depends on the weather.* | **Unit 135D** *(Index: p. 374)*<br>*(See also Unit 62A for depend on + -ing)* |
| **"Good in / bad in"**<br>*(Calque of ser bueno/malo en)* | **good at / bad at (+ noun / -ing)**<br>Competence, skills, and academic subjects take *at*. Contrast with *good of someone* (kindness) and *good to someone* (treatment).<br>• ❌ *She is very good in languages.*<br>• ✅ *She is very good at languages.* | **Unit 131B** & **Unit 60A** *(Index: pp. 373, 376)*<br>*(Contrast Unit 130A: good of/to)* |
| **"Married with / engaged with"**<br>*(Calque of casado con)* | **married to / engaged to**<br>The recipient partner requires *to*. Note: *married with* is only used when referring to children (*married with two kids*).<br>• ❌ *He got married with a doctor.*<br>• ✅ *He got married to a doctor.* | **Unit 131B** *(Index: p. 377)*<br>*(See also Unit 44D for get married)* |
| **"Interested for / interested to"**<br>*(From interesado en / por)* | **interested in (+ noun / -ing)**<br>Fields of interest require *in*. *Interested to do* is reserved strictly for reactions to incoming information (*interested to hear / know*).<br>• ❌ *I'm interested for learning Russian.*<br>• ✅ *I'm interested in learning Russian.* | **Unit 60A**, **Unit 66B**, **Unit 131B** *(Index: pp. 376, 377)* |
| **"Look forward to hear / meet"**<br>*(Using infinitive instead of gerund)* | **look forward to + -ing**<br>The *to* is a preposition, not an infinitive marker; therefore, it must be followed by a noun or gerund (*-ing*).<br>• ❌ *I look forward to meet you.*<br>• ✅ *I look forward to meeting you.* | **Unit 60C** & **Unit 62A** *(Index: pp. 376, 377)*<br>*(Contrast Unit 54 for verb + to-infinitive)* |
| **"Explain me something"**<br>*(Direct transfer of explicame algo)* | **explain something to somebody / explain to me**<br>*Explain* and *describe* cannot take an indirect object without *to*. The pattern *explain + person + thing* does not exist in English.<br>• ❌ *Can you explain me this rule?*<br>• ✅ *Can you explain this rule to me?* | **Unit 132A, 132B** *(Index: p. 375)*<br>*(Contrast Unit 55 for tell / ask sb)* |
| **"Listen music / listen the radio"**<br>*(Omitting preposition from escuchar)* | **listen to somebody / something**<br>*Listen* is an intransitive verb requiring *to* before any object. Contrast with *hear*, which is transitive and takes no preposition.<br>• ❌ *I like to listen music.*<br>• ✅ *I like to listen to music.* | **Unit 132A** *(Index: p. 377)*<br>*(Contrast Unit 4C & 67 for hear)* |
| **"Discuss about / mention about"**<br>*(Transfer of discutir sobre / hablar de)* | **discuss something (no preposition)**<br>*Discuss* takes a direct object without *about*. Contrast with the noun construction *have a discussion about*.<br>• ❌ *We need to discuss about the budget.*<br>• ✅ *We need to discuss the budget.* | **Unit 133A** *(Index: p. 375)*<br>*(Contrast Unit 133A: talk about)* |
| **"Pay the dinner / pay the coffee"**<br>*(Calque of pagar la comida)* | **pay for something vs pay somebody/bill**<br>Use *pay for* items, goods, meals, and services. Use *pay* directly without preposition only for people, bills, fines, rent, or debts.<br>• ❌ *I will pay the drinks.*<br>• ✅ *I will pay for the drinks.* | **Unit 135B** *(Index: p. 378)*<br>*(Contrast Unit 135B: pay the bill)* |
| **"I am agree / I am not agree"**<br>*(Literal transfer of estoy/no estoy de acuerdo)* | **I agree / I don't agree**<br>*Agree* is an active lexical verb, never a predicate adjective with *to be*. Negation uses auxiliary *do/does/did*.<br>• ❌ *I am completely agree with you.*<br>• ✅ *I completely agree with you.* | **Unit 2C, 2.5** *(Index: p. 373)*<br>*(See also Unit 54A & Unit 56A)* |
| **"Be used to do"**<br>*(Confusing soler with estar acostumbrado)* | **be / get used to + -ing vs used to + bare infinitive**<br>*Be/get used to + -ing* indicates familiarity/adaptation (*to* is a preposition). *Used to + base form* denotes discontinued past habits.<br>• ❌ *I am used to wake up early.*<br>• ✅ *I am used to waking up early.* | **Unit 61A–D** *(Index: p. 380)*<br>*(Contrast Unit 18A–F: used to do)* |
| **"He suggested me to go"**<br>*(Calque of me sugirió ir)* | **suggest + -ing OR suggest (that) sb (should) do**<br>*Suggest* never permits the pattern *verb + object + to-infinitive*. It requires a gerund or a that-clause (often with subjunctive or *should*).<br>• ❌ *She suggested me to apply for the job.*<br>• ✅ *She suggested that I (should) apply...* | **Unit 34A–C**, **Unit 53**, **Unit 55B** *(Index: p. 379)* |
| **"When I will arrive... / Until you will come"**<br>*(Future tense in temporal clauses)* | **when / as soon as / until / before + present simple**<br>Time clauses referring to future events strictly use present tenses (*simple or present perfect*), never *will*.<br>• ❌ *I'll call you when I will get home.*<br>• ✅ *I'll call you when I get home.* | **Unit 25A–D** *(Index: pp. 375, 380)*<br>*(See also Appendix 3.5)* |
| **"An advice / many informations / furnitures"**<br>*(Treating uncountable nouns as countables)* | **uncountable nouns (zero plural, zero a/an)**<br>Nouns such as *advice, information, furniture, luggage, baggage, news, weather, progress* are strictly non-count in English.<br>• ❌ *He gave me a good advice.*<br>• ✅ *He gave me some good advice (a piece of advice).* | **Unit 70A–B** *(Index: pp. 373, 376, 377, 379)*<br>*(Contrast Unit 69 for countable vs uncountable)* |
| **"The life is hard / I like the dogs"**<br>*(Generic use of Spanish artículo determinado)* | **Zero article with plural & uncountable nouns in general**<br>Do not use *the* when generalizing about classes, abstract concepts, or plural categories. *The* is reserved for specific referents.<br>• ❌ *The life has changed a lot.*<br>• ✅ *Life has changed a lot.* | **Unit 75A–B** *(Index: p. 379)*<br>*(Contrast Unit 72 & 73 for specific use of the)* |
| **"I live here since five years / from 2018"**<br>*(Spanish present + desde hace: Vivo aquí desde...)* | **Present perfect continuous / simple + for / since**<br>Unfinished actions continuing up to the present require the present perfect (*have been doing / have lived*), with *for* (duration) and *since* (starting point).<br>• ❌ *I live in London since three years.*<br>• ✅ *I have been living in London for three years.* | **Unit 11A–B**, **Unit 12A–D** *(Index: pp. 376, 379)*<br>*(See also Unit 9B & Unit 10)* |
| **"For to buy / for improve"**<br>*(Spanish para + infinitivo)* | **to + infinitive (infinitive of purpose)**<br>To express personal purpose or intention, English uses the *to-infinitive* (or *in order to / so that*). *For + -ing* is reserved for the general function of tools/objects.<br>• ❌ *I went to the shop for to buy bread.*<br>• ✅ *I went to the shop to buy bread.* | **Unit 64A–D** *(Index: pp. 376, 378)*<br>*(Contrast Unit 103C: too/enough + to...)* |
| **"I bought yesterday a book"**<br>*(Separating verb from its direct object)* | **Strict word order: Verb + Direct Object together**<br>Adverbs of time and place cannot split the verb and its direct object. The object must immediately follow the verb: *verb + object + place + time*.<br>• ❌ *I lost yesterday my keys.*<br>• ✅ *I lost my keys yesterday.* | **Unit 109A–B** *(Index: p. 380)*<br>*(Contrast Unit 110 for mid-position adverbs)* |
| **"I am knowing him well / I am understanding"**<br>*(Overextending continuous aspect to stative verbs)* | **Present simple for stative verbs**<br>Verbs of cognition, emotion, possession, and sensory perception (*know, like, understand, believe, want, consist, belong*) reject continuous forms.<br>• ❌ *I am not believing what he says.*<br>• ✅ *I don't believe what he says.* | **Unit 4A–C**, **Unit 6E**, **Unit 10D**, **Unit 16D** *(Index: pp. 375, 380)* |
| **"If I would have money, I bought..."**<br>*(Misusing would in if-clauses; Spanish si tuviera)* | **if + past simple / past perfect (no would in if-clause)**<br>Conditional *if*-clauses require the past simple (hypothetical present) or past perfect (hypothetical past). *Would* belongs exclusively in the result clause.<br>• ❌ *If I would know the answer, I would tell you.*<br>• ✅ *If I knew the answer, I would tell you.* | **Unit 38A–C**, **Unit 39A–E**, **Unit 40A–D** *(Index: pp. 375, 380)* |
| **"I wish I have / I wish you come"**<br>*(Using present tenses after wish; Spanish ojalá)* | **wish + past tense / could / would**<br>Regrets about present situations require the past tense (*I wish I knew*). Regrets about past events require past perfect (*I wish I had known*).<br>• ❌ *I wish I have more free time.*<br>• ✅ *I wish I had more free time.* | **Unit 41A–D** *(Index: p. 380)*<br>*(Contrast Unit 41A: wish vs hope)* |
| **"Hardly / hard" confusion**<br>*(Confusing adverb formation with Spanish duramente)* | **hard (adverb = intensely) vs hardly (adverb = scarcely/barely)**<br>*Hard* is both adjective and adverb (*work hard*). *Hardly* is a negative minimizer meaning "almost not" and precedes the main verb.<br>• ❌ *He works hardly all day.*<br>• ✅ *He works hard all day.* *(He hardly works = barely works)* | **Unit 101B–D** *(Index: p. 376)*<br>*(See also Unit 86 for hardly any)* |
| **"So" vs "Such" misapplication**<br>*(Direct transfer of tan vs tanto)* | **so + adjective/adverb vs such + (a/an) + (adj) + noun**<br>Use *so* before solitary adjectives or adverbs (*so tired, so quickly*). Use *such* before nouns or noun phrases (*such a nice day, such people*).<br>• ❌ *It was so long trip.*<br>• ✅ *It was such a long trip.* *(The trip was so long.)* | **Unit 102A–D** *(Index: p. 379)*<br>*(See also Unit 102 for so/such... that)* |
| **"During" vs "For" vs "While"**<br>*(Translating durante / mientras)* | **during + noun vs for + time period vs while + clause**<br>*During* specifies when something happens (*during the film*). *For* measures duration (*for two hours*). *While* introduces a clause with a verb (*while I was eating*).<br>• ❌ *I stayed in Paris during two weeks.*<br>• ✅ *I stayed in Paris for two weeks.* | **Unit 119A–C** *(Index: pp. 375, 376, 380)*<br>*(See also Unit 68B for while + -ing)* |
| **"In time" vs "On time"**<br>*(Collapsing distinct meanings into a tiempo)* | **on time (punctual/scheduled) vs in time (soon enough)**<br>*On time* means at the scheduled hour (*The train was on time*). *In time (for/to do)* means with enough time to spare before a deadline or event.<br>• ❌ *We arrived on time to catch the train.*<br>• ✅ *We arrived in time to catch the train.* | **Unit 122A** *(Index: pp. 376, 378)*<br>*(See also Unit 122A for just in time)* |
| **"At the end" vs "In the end"**<br>*(Collapsing distinct meanings into al final)* | **at the end (of something) vs in the end (finally/eventually)**<br>*At the end of* marks a specific terminal point in space or time (*at the end of the street/month*). *In the end* signals a final conclusion after difficulties.<br>• ❌ *In the end of the movie, he dies.*<br>• ✅ *At the end of the movie, he dies.* | **Unit 122B** *(Index: pp. 375, 376)*<br>*(See also Unit 124C for at the end of)* |
| **"By" vs "Until" confusion**<br>*(Translating para / hasta)* | **by (deadline: not later than) vs until (duration: up to a point)**<br>Use *by* when an action will happen at or before a deadline. Use *until* to describe a continuous state or action that continues up to a time boundary.<br>• ❌ *I have to finish the report until Friday.*<br>• ✅ *I have to finish the report by Friday.* | **Unit 120A–D** *(Index: pp. 374, 380)*<br>*(See also Unit 120D for by the time)* |
| **"Everything what happened / The thing what..."**<br>*(Translating lo que as what after antecedents)* | **that (relative pronoun) vs what (= the thing that)**<br>*What* cannot follow an expressed antecedent (*everything, all, the thing*). Use *that* (or zero pronoun) after quantifiers like *everything* or *something*.<br>• ❌ *Everything what he said was a lie.*<br>• ✅ *Everything that he said was a lie.* *(What he said was a lie.)* | **Unit 92D** & **Unit 93D** *(Index: pp. 378, 380)*<br>*(Contrast Unit 96C for what vs which)* |
| **"Turn off it / Put on them"**<br>*(Incorrect word order with separable phrasal verbs)* | **Verb + Pronoun + Particle (mandatory sandwich order)**<br>When the direct object of a separable phrasal verb is a pronoun (*it, them, him, her*), the pronoun must precede the particle.<br>• ❌ *The music is loud. Turn off it.*<br>• ✅ *The music is loud. Turn it off.* | **Unit 137D** *(Index: pp. 378, 380)*<br>*(See also Units 138–145)* |
| **"I also not / Me neither" in formal responses**<br>*(Transfer of yo tampoco)* | **Neither + auxiliary + subject (Neither do I / Nor can I)**<br>To agree with a negative statement, use *Neither/Nor + auxiliary verb + subject*. English rejects the literal transfer *I also not*.<br>• ❌ *'I don't like horror movies.' 'I also not.'*<br>• ✅ *'I don't like horror movies.' 'Neither do I.'* | **Unit 51C** *(Index: pp. 374, 377)*<br>*(Contrast Unit 51C: So do I for positives)* |

## 3. Complete Murphy Structural Syllabus (16 Sections, Units 1–145) Mapped to B2/C1 Competencies

This section outlines the 16 pedagogical sections of *English Grammar in Use*, highlighting the high-level focus areas and primary B2/C1 learning targets for each block.

### Section 1: Present and Past (Units 1–6)
- **Unit 1**: Present continuous (*I am doing*) — temporary situations, actions around now, changing/developing states (*getting, becoming*).
- **Unit 2**: Present simple (*I do*) — general truths, habits, performative verbs (*I promise, I suggest, I agree*).
- **Units 3–4**: Present continuous vs present simple comparisons — permanent vs temporary aspect; stative verbs (*know, understand, believe, belong*).
- **Unit 5**: Past simple (*I did*) — completed historical/past events with definitive time frames.
- **Unit 6**: Past continuous (*I was doing*) — interrupted actions in the past, simultaneous background actions.
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Eliminating stative-verb continuous forms (*I am knowing*) and avoiding present continuous for permanent routines.

### Section 2: Present Perfect and Past (Units 7–18)
- **Units 7–8**: Present perfect simple (*I have done*) — connection to now, recent news (*just, already, yet*), lifetime experience (*ever, never*).
- **Units 9–10**: Present perfect continuous (*I have been doing*) — ongoing activities, recently stopped actions with present evidence.
- **Units 11–12**: *How long have you (been)...?*, *for*, *since*, and *ago* — measuring open-ended duration vs closed past periods.
- **Units 13–14**: Present perfect vs past simple — definite past time adverbs (*yesterday, in 2015, when*) requiring past simple.
- **Units 15–16**: Past perfect simple and continuous (*I had done / had been doing*) — narrative sequencing ("past of the past").
- **Unit 17**: *Have* and *have got* — possession vs dynamic event collocations (*have lunch, have a chat*).
- **Unit 18**: *Used to (do)* — discontinued past states and habits.
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Eradicating the present tense for duration continuing to the present (*I live here since 2 years* $\rightarrow$ *I have lived / have been living here for 2 years*).

### Section 3: Future (Units 19–25)
- **Unit 19**: Present tenses (*I am doing / I do*) for future arrangements and official timetables.
- **Unit 20**: *Going to* — prior decisions and immediate physical evidence.
- **Units 21–22**: *Will* and *shall* — spontaneous decisions, offers, promises, objective predictions.
- **Unit 23**: *Will* vs *going to* contrast.
- **Unit 24**: Future continuous (*will be doing*) and future perfect (*will have done*).
- **Unit 25**: Time and conditional clauses (*when I do, as soon as I do, if I do*) — strict prohibition of *will* in temporal subordinate clauses.
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Mastering *will have done* by a deadline and suppressing *will* after *when, as soon as, until*.

### Section 4: Modals (Units 26–37)
- **Units 26–27**: *Can, could, (be) able to* — general ability vs specific achievement in the past (*was able to / managed to* vs *could*).
- **Unit 28**: *Must* and *can't* — present epistemic deduction.
- **Units 29–30**: *May* and *might* — possibility, epistemic speculation (*might have done*).
- **Units 31–32**: *Have to*, *must*, *mustn't*, *needn't* — external obligation vs personal compulsion; lack of obligation (*needn't / don't have to*) vs strict prohibition (*mustn't*).
- **Units 33–34**: *Should* — advice, expectations, and *should* in that-clauses after verbs like *suggest, insist, recommend* (subjunctive alternatives).
- **Unit 35**: *Had better* (urgent warning) and *it's time + past tense*.
- **Units 36–37**: *Would* and polite modal requests/offers.
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Differentiating *mustn't* (prohibited) from *don't have to* (optional), and using past modals of deduction (*must have been, can't have done*).

### Section 5: *If* and *Wish* (Units 38–41)
- **Unit 38**: Real vs unreal conditionals (*if I do* vs *if I did*).
- **Unit 39**: Second conditional (*if I knew... I would*); subjunctive *were* (*if I were you*).
- **Unit 40**: Third conditional (*if I had known... I would have*).
- **Unit 41**: *Wish* — present unreal states (*wish + past*), past regrets (*wish + past perfect*), and dissatisfaction with habits (*wish + would*).
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Eliminating *would* from the *if*-clause (*if I would have time* $\rightarrow$ *if I had time*).

### Section 6: Passive (Units 42–46)
- **Units 42–44**: Passive voice formations across simple, continuous, and perfect tenses; agent marker *by*.
- **Unit 45**: Impersonal reporting passive (*it is said that... / he is said to be... / he is supposed to*).
- **Unit 46**: Causative *have/get something done* (*I had my car repaired*).
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Using advanced reporting passive structures (*he is thought to have left*) and causative constructions instead of active calques.

### Section 7: Reported Speech (Units 47–48)
- **Units 47–48**: Backshifting of tenses; pronoun and time expression adjustments; reporting verbs (*say* vs *tell*; *warn, promise, encourage*).
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Remembering that *tell* requires a personal object (*he told me that...*), whereas *say* takes *to* (*he said to me that...*).

### Section 8: Questions and Auxiliary Verbs (Units 49–52)
- **Unit 49**: Subject vs object questions (*Who saw you?* vs *Who did you see?*); prepositions at the end of questions (*What are you looking at?*).
- **Unit 50**: Embedded indirect questions (*Do you know where he lives?* — normal subject-verb order, no auxiliary inversion).
- **Unit 51**: Auxiliary verbs in short answers, agreement (*So do I / Neither do I*).
- **Unit 52**: Question tags (*aren't you?, did they?, will you?*).
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Retaining declarative word order in indirect questions (*Do you know what time does the train leave?* $\rightarrow$ *what time the train leaves*).

### Section 9: *-ing* and *to...* (Verb Patterns) (Units 53–68)
- **Units 53–55**: Verb + *-ing* (*enjoy, avoid, mind*) vs Verb + *to...* (*decide, manage, afford*) vs Verb + object + *to...* (*want sb to do*).
- **Units 56–58**: Dual-pattern verbs with meaning changes (*remember, regret, stop, try, need*).
- **Unit 59**: *Prefer* and *would rather*.
- **Units 60–63**: Preposition + *-ing* (*interested in doing, look forward to doing, there's no point in doing*).
- **Units 64–66**: Purpose (*to...* vs *for*); adjective + *to...*; *afraid to do* vs *afraid of doing*.
- **Units 67–68**: Verbs of perception (*see sb do* vs *see sb doing*); participle clauses (*Having seen the report, he...*).
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Mastering verbs changing meaning with gerund vs infinitive (*stop smoking* vs *stop to smoke*) and preposition + gerund mastery.

### Section 10: Articles and Nouns (Units 69–81)
- **Units 69–71**: Countable vs uncountable nouns; *a/an* vs *some*.
- **Units 72–76**: Definite article *the* vs zero article; institutional places (*school / the school, prison / the prison*); generic vs specific statements (*life, music*).
- **Units 77–78**: Proper names, geographical features, streets, institutions with and without *the*.
- **Units 79–81**: Plural forms; collective nouns (*police, staff*); noun + noun compounds (*a two-hour flight*); genitive *-'s* vs *of...*.
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Suppressing *the* with plural and abstract concepts; treating singular measurement modifiers as singular (*a three-year-old child*, not *three years old*).

### Section 11: Pronouns and Determiners (Units 82–91)
- **Units 82–83**: Reflexive pronouns (*myself, yourself*); reciprocal *each other*; *a friend of mine*.
- **Unit 84**: *There...* vs *It...*.
- **Units 85–87**: *Some, any, no, none*; quantifiers (*much, many, little, few, plenty*).
- **Units 88–91**: *All / all of, most / most of*; *both / neither / either*; *each* and *every*.
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Correctly distinguishing *neither of* (two items) from *none of* (three or more items); agreement with indefinite pronouns (*everybody has their...*).

### Section 12: Relative Clauses (Units 92–97)
- **Units 92–94**: Defining relative clauses; subject vs object pronouns; omission of object pronouns; *whose, whom, where*.
- **Units 95–96**: Non-defining (extra-information) relative clauses with commas; prepositions in relative clauses (*with whom, of which*).
- **Unit 97**: Reduced relative clauses with *-ing* and *-ed* participles (*the man talking to Tom*).
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Never using *that* in non-defining relative clauses with commas (*My car, that is old...* $\rightarrow$ *which is old*); distinguishing *what* from *that*.

### Section 13: Adjectives and Adverbs (Units 98–112)
- **Units 98–99**: Participle adjectives (*bored* vs *boring*); adjective order before nouns (*opinion, size, age, shape, colour, origin, material*).
- **Units 100–104**: Adverb formation (*quick/quickly*); irregular adverbs (*fast, hard, well, late*); *so* vs *such*; *enough* and *too*.
- **Units 105–108**: Comparatives and superlatives (*cheaper, more expensive, the sooner the better*).
- **Units 109–110**: Syntactic word order: Verb + object adjacency; placement of mid-position adverbs (*always, also, already, probably*).
- **Units 111–112**: *Still, yet, already, any more*; focus adverb *even*.
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Placing adverbs of frequency/mid-position before main verbs but after *to be* (*I always have lived* $\rightarrow$ *I have always lived*).

### Section 14: Conjunctions and Prepositions (Units 113–120)
- **Unit 113**: Concession clauses: *although / even though* (+ clause) vs *in spite of / despite* (+ noun / -ing).
- **Unit 114**: *In case* (preventative contingency) vs *if*.
- **Unit 115**: *Unless* (= *if not*), *as long as, provided / providing*.
- **Units 116–118**: *As* (simultaneous time / reason); *like* vs *as*; *as if / as though*.
- **Unit 119**: *During* (+ noun) vs *for* (+ duration) vs *while* (+ clause).
- **Unit 120**: *By* (deadline) vs *until* (ongoing duration).
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Avoiding *despite of* (*despite* takes no preposition); using *in case* with present simple without *will*.

### Section 15: Prepositions (Units 121–136)
- **Units 121–122**: Time prepositions: *at, on, in*; *on time* vs *in time*; *at the end* vs *in the end*.
- **Units 123–128**: Position and movement prepositions: *in, at, on*; *to, into*; means of travel (*by bus* vs *on the bus*).
- **Unit 129**: Noun + preposition collocations (*reason for, key to, damage to, connection with/between*).
- **Units 130–131**: Adjective + preposition collocations (*proud of, ashamed of, keen on, typical of, good at*).
- **Units 132–136**: Verb + preposition collocations (*apologise to sb for sth, remind of/about, congratulate on, divide into, accuse of*).
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Eliminating literal translations from Spanish prepositions (*en, de, por, para*).

### Section 16: Phrasal Verbs (Units 137–145)
- **Unit 137**: Introduction to phrasal verbs, literal vs idiomatic meanings; separable vs inseparable syntax and pronoun placement rules.
- **Units 138–139**: Phrasal verbs with *in* and *out* (*drop in, join in, run out of, work out, carry out*).
- **Units 140–141**: Phrasal verbs with *on* and *off* (*put on/off, call off, see off, get on with, carry on*).
- **Units 142–144**: Phrasal verbs with *up* and *down* (*turn down, let down, break down, give up, end up, put up with*).
- **Unit 145**: Phrasal verbs with *away* and *back* (*get away with, give away, pay back, look back*).
- *Key B2/C1 Target*: Automatic pronoun inversion with separable particles (*give back it* $\rightarrow$ *give it back*).

- **Appendix 2: Present and past tenses** (p. 294) — comprehensive comparison matrix of simple vs continuous aspect across present and past.
- **Appendix 3: The future** (p. 295) — summary of all 5 future forms (*will, going to, present continuous, present simple, future continuous/perfect*).
- **Appendix 4: Modal verbs** (p. 296) — cross-cutting functional summary of modals for ability, permission, deduction, and obligation.
- **Appendix 5: Short forms / Contractions** (p. 297) — rules for unstressed spoken contractions and written apostrophes (*I'm, you'd, shouldn't*).
- **Appendix 6: Spelling** (p. 298) — rules for consonant doubling, *-ing* addition, and plural suffixes.
- **Appendix 7: American English** (p. 300) — key British vs American grammatical variations (*have got / have*, *gotten*, prepositions like *at/on the weekend*).
- **Study Guide** (pp. 326–335) — 16 modular multiple-choice diagnostic tests mapping student errors directly to their remediating Unit numbers.
- **Key to Exercises & Study Guide** (pp. 336–372) — complete model solutions for independent verification.
- **Comprehensive Index** (pp. 373–380) — exhaustive alphabetical lookup index citing exact unit numbers and sub-sections for every grammar word and construction.
